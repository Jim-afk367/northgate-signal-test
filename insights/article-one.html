Five signs your vibration sensors need recalibration

15 June 2026 · Northgate Signal Systems engineering team

Home Insights

Vibration sensors on rotating equipment are usually installed once and left alone for years. That is fine for the sensor itself in most cases, but the mounting, the cabling, and the surrounding thermal environment all drift over time, and any of those can quietly shift what your sensor is actually reporting. We see this most often on equipment that has been through a rebuild or a bearing replacement, where the sensor was removed and reinstalled without anyone re-baselining it against a known-good reading.

The first sign is usually a baseline reading that has moved without a corresponding change in the equipment's actual condition. If your overall vibration level has crept up by ten or fifteen percent over a few months with no maintenance events and no change in load, look at the mount before you look at the bearing. A loosened bracket or a cracked weld near the sensor can produce exactly this kind of slow, steady drift, and it is far cheaper to fix than the bearing failure it will eventually be blamed for.

The second sign is inconsistent readings between sensors that should be correlated. On a typical motor-pump pair, the drive-end and non-drive-end bearings usually move together when something changes upstream. If one sensor is reporting a fault signature and its neighbour is flat, it is worth checking the cable run and connector on the outlier before assuming the fault is real. Chafed cable insulation near a conduit edge is a common and unglamorous cause of readings that look like a mechanical problem but are actually an electrical one.

The third sign is a sensor that reports fine at idle but produces noisy data under load. This usually points to a thermal issue rather than a vibration issue: as the equipment heats up, expansion can change the mounting geometry just enough to introduce noise that was not there at startup. If you have temperature logging alongside vibration, overlay the two and look for the noise appearing right around the point where the temperature curve flattens out.

The fourth sign is a step change in reported values immediately after any maintenance event, even one that had nothing to do with the sensor. Technicians working near a sensor to service an unrelated component can bump a bracket or partially disconnect a cable without noticing. It is worth making sensor verification a standard step after any work order that touches the same equipment, not just work orders that mention the sensor directly.

The fifth sign is simply age. Piezoelectric sensors have a long service life, but connectors, cable jackets, and mounting adhesive do not last forever, especially in washdown or high-temperature environments. If a sensor has been in place for more than five years in a harsh environment, a physical inspection during the next planned outage is worth the ten minutes it takes, even if the data still looks reasonable.

None of these checks require new hardware. They require someone to look at the sensor and its mounting, not just the dashboard, on a schedule that matches how the plant actually uses the equipment.
