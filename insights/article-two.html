Choosing the right sensor sampling rate for predictive maintenance

10 July 2026 · Northgate Signal Systems engineering team

Home Insights

Sampling rate is one of the first decisions a plant engineering team makes when instrumenting rotating equipment, and it is easy to get wrong in either direction. Sample too slowly and you miss the early harmonics that distinguish a developing bearing fault from normal wear. Sample too fast and you generate more data than your storage, your network, or your analysts can realistically absorb, and most of it never gets looked at.

The starting point is the equipment's running speed and the highest-order fault frequency you actually care about detecting. For most bearing fault signatures, you need enough bandwidth to resolve harmonics up to roughly ten times the shaft rotation frequency, and Nyquist means your sampling rate needs to be at least double that. In practice, we recommend building in a comfortable margin beyond the theoretical minimum, both because real bearings produce fault energy across a wider band than the textbook formula suggests, and because a margin gives you headroom if the equipment's operating speed changes.

Gearbox monitoring adds a wrinkle, because gear mesh frequencies are typically much higher than shaft rotation frequencies and often carry the earliest evidence of tooth wear in sidebands around the mesh frequency rather than at the mesh frequency itself. If your sampling rate was chosen only around the shaft speed, you may be aliasing exactly the signal you most need. This is the single most common sampling mistake we see in the field: a sensor spec sized for the slow shaft, deployed on equipment where the interesting fault signature lives an order of magnitude higher.

Storage cost is the practical constraint that usually settles the argument. Continuous high-rate sampling across a large fleet of sensors adds up quickly, which is why most production deployments use a triggered or scheduled sampling scheme rather than continuous streaming: a lower-rate always-on trend signal for early warning, combined with short high-rate bursts captured either on a schedule or when the trend signal crosses a threshold. This gives you the resolution you need for diagnosis without paying the storage and bandwidth cost of high-rate sampling around the clock.

The right answer depends on the equipment, not on a rule of thumb that ignores it. A slow-turning conveyor pulley and a high-speed spindle motor have almost nothing in common in their fault frequency content, and a sampling scheme sized for one will either waste resources or miss faults on the other. Start from the fault frequencies you need to resolve, build in margin, and only then work backward to a sampling and storage budget you can sustain.
